Showcase · Kestrel

A robot arm, designed by an AI agent in Polykrom, then checked by everything but its word.

Seven plain-language briefs, one agent, Polykrom's MCP tools and nothing else. Kestrel is a desktop pick-and-place arm: a gear-driven turntable, shoulder and elbow, a coupled parallel gripper, standard hardware from the library, a motion study, a stress study and a robot description for simulators. Every number below was re-measured on the saved design by an independent script. The last section lists what this version can't do yet.

How it was built

It caught four mistakes in our own brief

The briefs were written quickly, on purpose, the way people actually ask. The agent built what was asked, measured it, and reported where the brief couldn't work. It never quietly changed the design to hide a problem.

The motor didn't fit

A 42.3 mm NEMA 17 centred 60 mm out reaches 81.15 mm, past the 66 mm inside wall of a Ø140 base. It found the overlap (6,194 mm³) and offered three fixes. We enlarged the base.

The bearing seat broke through

A 7 mm pocket in a 4 mm top is a hole, not a seat. We added a hub under it.

Tapped holes went right through

8 mm deep in an 8 mm disc. They became 6 mm, leaving 5.2 mm of thread engaged for the M4 screws.

The cheeks covered the screw holes

Half of each yoke-plate hole sat under a cheek, so no screw head could seat. It shortened the cheeks from 40 to 28 mm, checked that the arm still clears them, and explained the alternative.

Is the upper arm strong enough?

The brief asked for SF ≥ 3 and less than 0.2 mm deflection, with 40 N square to the arm at the elbow, its worst case. The first result was SF 43. So in stage 7 the agent was asked to make it as light as it reasonably could without touching any fit. It ran the solver after every change.

Linear static FEA through Nightfall (Netgen mesh, MFEM solve). The final run was converged over three meshes down to 0.89 mm: deflection 0.1281 / 0.1283 / 0.1284 mm. The fillets were added because the peak stress kept rising with mesh refinement at the sharp steps. An independent re-run of the saved part gave SF 10.75, 25.7 MPa peak and 0.128 mm. The fits stayed exactly as they were: Ø8.200 holes at 140.000 mm, R12 ends, full 20 mm thickness at the pins.

Where this version falls short

Found by building Kestrel, not by guessing. Several were fixed the same day; the rest are on the roadmap.

Fit sweeps

A joint sweep can't turn a pin in a hole with 0.1 mm of clearance. It runs out of its collision budget, even for that one pair.

That's exactly the fit a sweep exists to check. The agent swept the other part pairs one at a time and checked the pins statically instead. → #25

Motion checks

Checking a 21-body study for collisions, all pairs at once, stops at step 0.

The work budget runs out, and any part that overlaps from the start (a thread in a tapped hole) eats it. The agent split the check into groups and sampled arm-against-base every 4th step. Pairs that touch at the start should be set aside, as joint sweeps already do. → #25

Limits

Joint limits are per joint, and nothing re-checks them when the assembly changes.

Each limit is safe on its own, but shoulder +114.5° with elbow −77.4° drives the forearm 2,661 mm³ into the yoke. There are no coupled limits or reachable-workspace check yet.

The elbow's −77.4° limit was swept in stage 2, before the gripper existed. Once the gripper was on, the wrist hit the upper arm at −67.7°. Polykrom didn't flag the stale limit; the agent found it in stage 7. It is the one value we changed by hand after the build. → #25

Kernel

The kernel refused 1 mm fillets along the upper arm's web-to-flange edges, so those stay sharp.

Contact checks that run on meshes also raised a false 0.022 mm "contact" on a pin with a true 0.100 mm gap, from the mesh's 0.16 mm chord tolerance. The exact check was right. → #25

Parts library

Library parts arrive with a placeholder material and bodies named Body1–9.

The 608 bearing seated facing out of its pocket and had to be flipped. It is a solid envelope, so in steel it weighs 18 g against a real bearing's 12 g. The screws seated on the plate rather than on their washers and were raised 0.8 mm by hand. → #43

Simulators

The URDF has no <mimic> tags.

The gear between pinion and turntable, and the link between the jaws, are lost in simulators. Effort and velocity limits fall back to defaults because parts don't carry actuator specs yet. → #27

Results on the part

Stress comes back as numbers, not colours on the part.

Nightfall now writes the surface field. Drawing it on the body is in progress. → #29

Gears

Gears are pitch-diameter blanks, not teeth.

The kinematics are exact, but there's no tooth contact or backlash to check. → #26

Visuals

The exported animation is flat grey.

The headless renderer ignores material appearance, so the 3D view above is the better picture. → #33

Fixed today

Building the earlier test assemblies found three bugs, all fixed:

  • A screw couldn't seat on a washer that was itself seated: the insert mixed up world and part coordinates.
  • A joint's named face could resolve on another part modelled at the same local coordinates.
  • An occurrence couldn't be named by its component.

→ #17
